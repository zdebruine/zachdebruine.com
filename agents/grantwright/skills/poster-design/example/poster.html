The clinical genetics gap.01 · Why now

The literature already contains the answer. The chart already contains the question. The omics already exists. None of them talk to each other.

Thousands of published case reports describe variant-to-phenotype relationships in exquisite detail. The patient’s chart already holds the phenotype, growth curves, family history, and prior testing. Single-cell genomics has produced an enormous evidence base for what variants do to cells.

But the literature is locked in PDFs. The chart is locked in EHR systems. The omics is locked in nine incompatible file formats spread across a dozen pipelines. Every clinical AI project starts by building the same plumbing twice.

Design principle
“The chart already contains the diagnosis. Our job is to make sure the model has read enough literature to recognize it — and the right omics to confirm it.”

From cells to clinic.02 · Bridge

Harmonization is the prerequisite for clinical intelligence. The same architecture that scales up to a billion research cells must scale down to a single patient’s chart. When every input parses the same way, the model can finally reason about phenotype rather than file format.

That is the bridge: research-grade harmonization, clinic-grade reasoning. Every layer of the framework in the central panel sits on the substrate described to the right.

A framework for clinical genetics AI.03 · Framework

Most clinical AI starts with the genome and tries to predict the phenotype. We invert that. Start with the literature; let case reports teach the model what a phenotype looks like; only then read the omics.

L1

Phenotype foundation — learned from the literature

Pre-train on every published clinical genetics case report. The model learns what a Loeys–Dietz looks like, what a 22q11.2 deletion presents like, what the natural history of an ASXL3 variant actually is — from thousands of phenotyped patients. A contrastive embedding space replaces brittle HPO classification.

↓
L2

Patient context — the chart, structured

HPO terms, growth curves, imaging, prior tests, family history, clinical notes. Assembled from the EHR into a single case-report-shaped input the L1 model already knows how to read.

↓
L3

Omics as evidence — not as the question

Variants, expression, methylation, splicing, single-cell programs — supplied as retrieval context, weighted by what the literature says matters for this phenotype. Inference chain: variant → cis-effect → NMF program → cell state → phenotype.

↓
L4

Answer — consistent with the case-report record

Every claim cites a paper, a chart finding, or a variant. The output reads like a differential a colleague would write, not a confidence score. Mechanistic, not embedding-distance.

Worked example · KCNQ1 variant of uncertain significance
“My patient has a novel variant in KCNQ1 — what should I expect?”
Answer. This variant disrupts a repolarization program active in ventricular cardiomyocytes and Purkinje fibers, with predicted effects on action-potential duration consistent with Long QT Syndrome Type 1. Similar perturbation signatures observed in 14 published single-cell datasets and 7 case reports. Suggested workup: baseline ECG with corrected QT, exercise stress test, family history of syncope/sudden cardiac death, cardiology referral for consideration of beta-blocker therapy. Confidence: HIGH.
Worked example · BMP-pathway craniofacial variant
Variant predicted to disrupt a BMP-pathway program active in cranial neural crest. Suggested workup: 3D CT craniofacial, echocardiogram — BMP-pathway variants associated with cardiac septal defects in 23% of cases. Evidence: 14 supporting single-cell datasets, 7 published case reports. Confidence: HIGH.
